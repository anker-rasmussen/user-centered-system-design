# User centered system design
## Session 1
Interactive systems and technologies are all around us nowadays. Human Computer Interaction (HCI) is a discipline concerned with the design, evaluation, and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use and with the study of major phenomena surrounding them (ACM SIGCHI, 1992).

HCI is interdisciplinary, drawing on Computer Science, Cognitive Psychology & Ergonomics, Design & Arts, and Social & Organisational Psychology.

Technology should provide good usability and leave the user feeling a positive user experience.

Users and developers should understand the context of the software they use and create.

Successful user-facing technology is created by understanding the goals and behaviors of the current users, by including them in the design process.

It's important to iterate on designs by progressively improving testing with users and learning from the evaluations.

Interactive systems deal with the transmission, display, storage, or transformation of information. Technologies range from components, devices, products, and software systems. Devices and systems that respond dynamically are included, but so are cameras, buildings, etc.

Devices that allow users to create inputs and initiate actions are *input*

Devices that show the data that has been input and what has happened to it (presenting results) are *output*

### Interaction types
- **Instructing** -- issuing commands to a system (e.g. typing, selecting menu options, pressing buttons)
- **Conversing** -- interacting with a system as if having a conversation (e.g. voice assistants, chatbots)
- **Manipulating** -- interacting with objects in a virtual or physical environment (e.g. dragging, zooming, rotating)
- **Exploring** -- moving through a virtual environment or information space (e.g. navigating a website, VR world)
- **Responding** -- the system takes the initiative and the user responds (e.g. notifications, alerts, prompts)

### Choosing technologies
The suitability of a technology depends on:
- **Task fit** -- does it support what the user needs to do?
- **User characteristics** -- abilities, experience, preferences
- **Context of use** -- where and when it will be used
- **Errors** -- does it minimise the likelihood and impact of errors?
- **Time** -- does it allow the user to complete tasks efficiently?

### Usability
The extent to which a product can be used to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use.

- User

Systems that have poor usability cause

- Inefficiencies in workflows, causing costs and user errors

- Systems that are underused!

## Session 2

### System centered -> User centered

In 1985 there was a paradigm shift, moving from what can the system do to what does the user need to do, and how can the UI help them accomplish their goals?

**Gould & Lewis (1985)** identified three key principles:
1. **Early focus on users** -- understand who will use the system and involve them from the start
2. **Empirical measurement** -- observe and measure how real users respond to prototypes and scenarios
3. **Iterative design** -- design is revised and refined based on evaluation results

### Process
How design work is organized and delivered
Several different frameworks exist, and there's no single "correct" one. This is because they emphasise different priorities and have their own drawbacks and advantages.

They can be used in conjunction or adapted.

### Key activities
| Phase | Stage Name | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Understand and specify the context of use** | User research | Find out about users (who, what, when, where, and how). |
| **Define design goals and requirements** | Definition | Derive high-level goals and specify constraints. |
| **Produce design solutions** | Conceptual design (ideation) & Detailed design (Prototyping) | Ideation and concept generation; development and design with prototyping. |
| **Evaluate design** | Evaluation | Test/evaluate what's being designed, both during and throughout the process. |



## Designing is an iterative process

There are cycles of designing, evaluating, and researching, as well as improving and eliminating alternative designs. Design won't be perfect the first time.

Alternative designs are tested individually and against each other. Give your ideas competition.

Design involves conceptual and detailed design; explore lots of ideas to get the concept right, then get the detail done right. (Explore a lot of designs!)

### Observing users
- Direct observation; watch them using your tool

    - Valuable technique for finding out what people do rather than what they say they do.
    - Yields detailed information esp. for verbalisation
    - Supplement with other techniques such as interviews.
- Where?
    - In the field
    - Natural environment
    - In the lab
    - Controlled environment
- Naturalistic observation; watch them use your tool in a real world setting
    - Researcher does not manipulate the activity
    - Researcher does not manipulate the setting

## Session 3
### Query techniques
Interviews
Questionnaires

Self reporting techniques: Researcher asks questions and records participants' responses.

Questions attempt to elicit
 - Facts
 - Behavior
 - Beliefs/attitudes/opinions

 Humans are good at facts. Normally these are easy to answer

 Behavior is a little harder for people to answer. Unreliable narrator

 Opinions/beliefs/attitudes are subjective and differ from person to person

 Answers may not truly reflect what the person believes (they may under/overreport).

 **Over-reporting** (social desirability): being a good citizen, seat belt usage, being well-informed, charitable giving, voting.

 **Under-reporting**: illness/disabilities, illegal behaviour, financial status, alcohol use.

 Questions can be seen as threatening, leading participants to report incorrectly.

When designing an interview/questionnaire, be clear about your purpose.

What do you want to know? Why? What can you ask? Why do you want or need that info?

For any question, ask "WHY do I want to know this?"

Be very specific:

How do you use the city website? <- BAD

What did you do on the City website the last time you used it? <- GOOD!

Don't bloat with unnecessary questions!

Use simple and unambiguous phrasing and vocabulary, and avoid asking multiple questions (conjoined).

Avoid leading words and loaded phrasing.

Start with easy questions that reassure them.

But make sure to ask the important questions early enough.


### Open ended or Closed ended questions?

- Open ended: No restrictions in response
- Closed ended (or fixed alternative); users must pick one of several
- Scale. Variant of closed ended where respondent must respond on a pre-determined scale (rating to the extent they agree/disagree with something)

| | Open-ended questions | Closed-ended questions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Detail** | Good for eliciting rich detail | Answers aren't very rich in detail |
| **Flexibility** | Can reveal unanticipated info | Require certainty about the range of possible answers — inflexible |
| **Early design** | Very useful in early design | Not so useful in early stages of design |
| **Evaluation** | Can also be useful in evaluation | More useful in evaluation, e.g. for rating or measuring things |
| **Comparison** | | Useful for comparison |
| **Effort** | Require more effort to answer — can be problematic in a questionnaire | |
| **Analysis** | Data captured is more difficult to analyse | Use simple statistics to analyse |

### Interviews

Are a purposeful conversation. Initiated by the interviewer to obtain research-relevant information. Useful for obtaining difficult information not easily accessible via questionnaires. Helpful in doing follow ups to understand a participant's mentality. Responses generate rich quality data.

Less good for capturing what people actually *do* (vs what they *say* they do). Can be time-consuming. Danger of researcher bias influencing the conversation.

#### Interview types
- **Structured** -- pre-determined questions in a fixed order; consistent but inflexible
- **Semi-structured** -- questions prepared in advance but the interviewer can be flexible with order and follow-up
- **Unstructured** -- no pre-determined questions; free conversation guided by the topic area

#### Planning and conducting interviews
- Create an **interview guide** (a plan of topics and questions)
- Start with a **lead statement** (introduce yourself, explain the purpose, set expectations)
- Begin with **warmup questions** (easy, non-threatening)
- Maintain a **logical flow** through the topics
- Close with a generic open question: *"Is there anything else you want to tell me?"*

#### Tips for effective interviewing
- Ask straightforward, succinct questions
- Avoid leading questions or cues to desired answers
- Don't pre-empt the participant's answers
- Acknowledge responses neutrally
- Be mindful of non-verbal behaviour (yours and theirs)
- Use **neutral probing** -- ask follow-up questions without suggesting an answer
- Use **paraphrasing** -- repeat back in your own words to confirm understanding
- Use **focusing/redirecting** -- gently steer back to the topic if they go off track
- Ground questions in **specific** experiences (not hypothetical)
- Slow down -- don't rush
- Allow silence -- give participants time to think

Ask straightforward, succinct, non-jargon, non-threatening questions.

Avoid leading questions or cues to desired answers. 

Seek specific answers.

Sampling (who is target population?)
Sample size! (quantitative; more is better), but greater variation, the more you have to ask

How will you find the right people (not asking will result in misleading data!)

### Questionnaires

Self-reporting instrument where respondents answer questions themselves. Can be delivered online (e.g. Qualtrics) or paper-based.

- **Recall** questions ask respondents to generate an answer from memory (harder)
- **Recognition** questions ask respondents to choose from given options (easier)

### Measurement scales

- **Nominal** -- categories with no inherent order (e.g. gender, colour, yes/no)
- **Ordinal** -- ordered categories but with uneven intervals between them (e.g. Likert scale: strongly disagree to strongly agree)
- **Interval** -- equal intervals between values but no true zero (e.g. temperature in Celsius)
- **Ratio** -- equal intervals with a true zero point (e.g. time, weight, distance)

### Questionnaire analysis
- **Scaled data** (ordinal/interval) -- calculate means, run t-tests
- **Categorical data** (nominal) -- calculate frequencies, run chi-square tests
- **Open-ended responses** -- code into categories, then analyse as categorical data

### Validated questionnaires
Questionnaires that have been tested and shown to have high **reliability** (consistent results) and **internal consistency** (items measure the same underlying construct). Using validated questionnaires strengthens your research findings.

### Recipe
- What is needed (goal)?
- Who?
- What method?
- Conduct a search for existing questions.
- Draft new questions
- Put questions in sequence
- Format the questionnaire
- Get peers to evaluate
- Pilot (get people to complete while thinking out loud)
- Fix (iterate) and release!

## Session 4
### Analysing and representing user data

We get data from evaluations, user research, and more in many formats. We need to:
- Provide evidence for findings
- Communicate findings to others
- Distil findings into easily digestible material
- Reflect on findings to provide recommendations

It's important to understand *why* you're analysing:
- To describe users for informing design?
- To identify problems with a view to fixing them?
- To identify opportunities for design?
- To identify needs, requirements, or constraints?
- To represent user tasks?

### Types of data

**Qualitative data** -- rich/detailed, e.g. comments in interviews, think-aloud transcripts, images, video.

**Quantitative data** -- things that can be turned into numbers or frequencies, e.g. scales, task measures.

Data collection may produce both: e.g. an interview captures qualitative data that can later be transformed into quantitative data (frequency of themes), or a questionnaire with Likert scales (quantitative) and open-ended answers (qualitative).

### Simple quantitative analysis
- Usually for questionnaire or observation data
- Calculate descriptive statistics (counts, averages, standard deviations)
- Run statistical tests -- e.g. t-test, ANOVA, Pearson's correlation -- but only if the sample size is big enough
- Produce graphs (bar chart, pie chart, etc.)
- Describe data appropriately -- e.g. don't report percentages for small numbers of participants
- Tools: Excel, SPSS

### Qualitative analysis (thematic analysis)
1. Familiarise yourself with the qualitative data you gathered
2. Look for recurring patterns -- similar things people say, do, or describe (inductive or deductive)
3. "Code" the data accordingly -- mark up parts of text where these patterns are observed
4. Review your coding -- iterate and adjust
5. Look for potential themes within your coding that may be relevant to your project
6. You can also turn this into simple frequencies to check the prevalence of codes/themes

### Affinity mapping

Organising data into groups or themes that are useful. Helps to identify patterns.

Process:
1. Write out important details or insights (e.g. activities, tasks, attitudes, motivations, skills) from the research onto individual sticky notes
2. Group the sticky notes into categories you feel make sense
3. Adjust the categories as new details/insights are uncovered
4. Review the categories and use them to inform design insights
5. Keep a record of the categories and their content for future reference

Flexible method -- can be done as a team or individually, in-person (sticky notes) or digitally (Miro, FigJam, LucidChart). Can use colour for different purposes (different users, different interview questions).

Useful for:
- Analysing data from interviews or observations
- Extracting themes and insights
- Synthesising research
- Identifying patterns, requirements, and priorities
- Generating personas
- Ideation
- Synthesising findings from usability testing


### Empathy maps

Descriptive representations of what you have discovered about the user(s) through qualitative research.

Four quadrants: **Says**, **Thinks**, **Does**, **Feels**.

Also captures **Pains** (fears, frustrations, obstacles, difficulties, challenges) and **Gains** (goals, hopes, measures of success).

Can represent a single user or an aggregation of users (start with individual maps, then aggregate).

To create an empathy map:
1. Collect qualitative data about users
2. Review data for each user to pull out findings for each segment (sticky notes help)
3. Organise on the empathy map

Uses:
- Organise findings from user research
- Create common ground in the design team
- Create empathy during subsequent design activities and help with decision making

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pros** | • Easy technique to learn and use.<br>• Organises detailed qualitative data from real people.<br>• Can represent single user or aggregated types/segments. |
| **Cons** | • Can become unmanageable if containing a lot of data.<br>• Overlaps can be frustrating.<br>• Harder to populate some quadrants from certain research.<br>• Important detail (e.g., chronology) is often missing.<br>• Focuses more on organisation than deep analysis. |
| **Watchpoints** | • Represent raw data, not researcher assumptions/inferences.<br>• Ensure you intentionally capture specific data types (like feelings). |

### User journey maps

A user journey is the end-to-end experience a person goes through as they try to achieve a goal -- a sequence of steps over time, from a starting point towards a particular goal/outcome, usually involving some kind of system or service.

A user journey *map* is one way of representing that experience. Based on data collected, can represent single or multiple users.

- **Current** user journey maps (aka 'as-is') -- what users do now *(user research)*
- **Future** user journey maps -- what users might do in the future *(conceptual design / envisionment)*

What is in a user journey map:
- **Context** -- where is the user? What external factors impact their use?
- **Progression** -- how does each step enable the user to get to the next?
- **Devices** -- what technology are they using?
- **Functionality** -- what features are they using?
- **Emotion** -- what is the user's emotional state at each step?
- **Pain points** -- what frustrations, challenges, or difficulties do they experience?
- **Opportunities** -- what opportunities for improvement might there be?

Represents a specific sequence of user activities (not all possible sequences). Shows interactions with different touchpoints. Shows context and user emotions. Can be high-level or very specific.

Creating a user journey map -- start rough, begin with a sketch, workshop it (sticky notes are good), iterate:
- Who is the user?
- What is the user goal?
    - Scope? Level of detail?
    - Journey start and endpoint?
- Main phases?
- Tasks and activities?
- What are they interacting with?
- Thinking or feeling during these?
- Pain points? Challenges? Wins?
- Opportunities to enhance?

#### Data sources for UJMs
- Observations
- Interviews
- Diary studies
- Support logs

#### UJM pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- |
| Useful communication tool | Only as good as the data behind them |
| Visual overview of the user experience | Not one-size-fits-all |
| Helps understand user tasks, behaviour, and needs | Risk of focusing on the company's perspective over the user's |
| Flexible in scope and level of detail | Need to be kept up to date |
| Good for identifying pain points, gaps, and opportunities | |

### Personas

Concrete representations of anticipated users
- Distinct types of users for a specific product
- Synthesised from data collected about real people
- Differentiated in terms of needs and behaviors (not trivial or superficial)
- Represented as individuals (but aren't real people)
- Information should be actionable
- Not just a summary of collected data!

Personas
- Represent usergroups
- Composite archetypes
- Don't design for *everyone*, pick the main usergroups
- Identify best key groups
- Differentiated in terms of needs and behaviors
- Make sure info is actionable!
- Should have one for each user group
    - Primary and secondary persona
    - Keep the number of personas to a minimum
- **Antipersonas** -- represent undesirable users or users for whom you are explicitly *not* designing (also used to consider potential harm)
- **Proto-personas** -- hypotheses about user types, created before research is complete. Useful as a starting point but must be validated.

#### What's in a persona
- **Goals and motivations** -- what they want to achieve
- **Frustrations and pain points** -- what gets in their way
- **Factors influencing behaviour** -- context, skills, attitudes, constraints
- **Enrichment details** -- name, photo, brief background to make them feel real

#### Persona goals and tasks (Alan Cooper)
- **Experience goals** -- how they want to *feel* (e.g. confident, in control, not stressed)
- **End goals** -- the motivation behind the specific task (e.g. get the best deal)
- **Life goals** -- what they want to achieve in life (e.g. live independently)

#### Using personas
1. As the 'user' in user journey maps
2. As the focus of ideation sessions
3. For expert reviews of prototypes

#### Persona watchpoints
- Keep them relevant and focused
- Don't bloat with unnecessary detail
- Watch for stereotyping and bias

### Coursework notes
Only make one persona.


## Session 5
### From user research to conceptual design

User research tells you about people, what they do, where they do it, etc. From there you need to define the design goal(s) or challenge(s) from the user's perspective.

Think about **problems** (e.g. users struggle with X) and **opportunities** (e.g. create a technology that will enable Y). Remember that *you* (the designer), not the users, will think about problems and opportunities based on what you discovered through user research.

### Strategy and scope

- Use user research to empathise with users and identify their needs/goals
- **Defining** the problem sharpens the strategy and begins to outline scope
    - What are we addressing? For whom? What is required?
- From abstract to more concrete, including articulating the goals for design

### Point of View (PoV) statements

Also called user need statements or problem statements. Design goals/challenges articulated from the perspective of user need and value.

Format: **[User]** needs **[Need]** so that **[Insight/Goal]**

Example: *Mrs Morton needs support remembering to take her medication at the right times, so that she can manage her health conditions safely and consistently.*

Note that users may not have *articulated* a need -- you may have identified a pain point or an opportunity to do something new/different.

PoV statements are:
- Grounded in research findings
- Shaped by project context
- Non-solution-specific
- Focused on outcomes

PoV statements are NOT a persona, a feature, or a screen.

They can be applied to varying scopes (parent/umbrella vs child) and help articulate the design goal(s)/challenge(s) your project will address.

### Requirements

Requirements are statements that specify what a product must do and the conditions it must meet. They translate user needs and project goals into clear, testable expectations of:
- **Behaviour** (what it must do)
- **Quality** (how well it must do it)
- **Constraints** (limits it must operate within)

Sources: user research, stakeholders, business goals, domain experts, standards/regulation, competitor/market analysis.

Compared with PoV/user-need statements, requirements are usually more system-centred than user-focused.

Types of requirements:
- **Functional requirements** -- what the product must do (capabilities/features)
- **Non-functional requirements** -- how well it must do it (quality attributes and constraints), e.g.:
    - Performance
    - Usability & UX
    - Security & privacy
    - Reliability and safety
    - Maintainability and portability

Each requirement should be traceable to a finding from your research. For coursework, prioritise key requirements in the main body (focus on what *must* be true -- outcomes + key constraints) and put extras in an appendix.

### User stories

From Agile software development. A short, simple description of a feature told from the perspective of the person who desires it.

Format: **As a** [user type] **I would like** [some action/goal] **so that** [some reason/benefit]

"Three Cs": Card, Conversation, Confirmation (Jeffries, 2001). A lightweight placeholder to use in discussion at the appropriate time (instead of detailed upfront specification). Usually stored/managed in a product backlog.

In design work:
- Collaboration tool -- a prompt for discussion
- Useful as a lightweight way to express capabilities/value
- Can be written at different sizes: epics (big) -> stories (smaller)
- Early on, keep them outcome/value-focused (avoid UI specifics)

Example: *"As a student, I would like to receive reminders of coursework deadlines, so that I remember to submit my coursework in time."*

### User story mapping

A way to organise stories into a coherent view of the product (map out product features). Helps teams see the "big picture" and prioritise what to build first.

Typical structure: **Activities** (high-level tasks) -> **Steps** (to complete the activity) -> **Details** (discrete interactions to complete a step)

### Conceptual design

Conceptual design is high-level design work. It bridges the *problem space* and the *design space*. Focuses on what kind of product or system it is and what facilities it must offer -- not the interface details.

- Begin by generating lots of design ideas
- Ideas are evaluated and refined, with weaker ones discarded
- Design is iterative -- exploration and elimination of alternatives is repeated
- You seldom get it right the first time -- the first idea is rarely the best, so give it competition!

Aims:
- Generate, explore & compare alternative high-level design ideas
- Envision users' future activities and contexts of use
- Make early design decisions about modes of interaction, types of interface, tasks to support, key concepts and workflows
- Gather early feedback to refine concepts

### Conceptual vs detailed design

**Conceptual design** -- high-level; focuses on what the system is and does. Concerned with users, goals, and context. Defines what the system will do, interaction types, interface types. Not about screen layout or labels.

**Detailed design** -- screen-level; focuses on how the system will look and behave. Defines interface layout, navigation, and visual details. Specifies functional logic and interaction flow.

### Design inspiration

Finding great ideas is a really hard part of the design process. Many people do one design and refine it because they can't think of alternatives. You can deliberately collect interesting design ideas *before* you need them, so you have inspiring material to draw on.

Sources: output from user research, similar projects/systems, different projects/systems, design libraries, ideation techniques.

### Ideation

The structured, creative process of generating ideas. Two interdependent uses:
- Early ideation to discover conceptual directions *(What could we design?)*
- Later ideation to explore different ways to realise the chosen concept -- help refine and elaborate it *(How might the system work in more detail?)*

This process is iterative, not strictly sequential.

Techniques:
- Brainstorming
- SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange)
- Worst possible idea
- Analogical reasoning
- Constraint removal
- Crazy 8s

### Brainstorming

A group activity to produce lots of ideas around a given problem. A mix of stakeholders working collaboratively, usually with a facilitator.

Elements of a successful brainstorm:
- Pick the right environment
- Have a facilitator
- Set a time limit
- Begin with a target problem/brief
- Allow one conversation at a time
- Refrain from judgment/criticism
- Encourage weird and wacky ideas
- Aim for quantity
- Build on others' ideas
- Stay visual
- Beware extroverts dominating / introverts not being heard
- Run a warmup activity beforehand

### "How Might We" questions

Rephrase your PoV statements into "How Might We" questions to use as a guide for discussion.

e.g. *"How might we remind Mrs Morton to take her medication?"*

These can then be used in brainstorming and other ideation sessions. A good way to frame challenges as opportunities for innovation.

## Session 6
### Conceptualising and contextualising

### Crazy 8s (rapid ideation)

A core Design Sprint method, part of the Sketch stage. A rapid sketching exercise: 8 rough sketches in 8 minutes (1 minute per sketch). Usually preceded by warm-up activities.

Why do it?
- Generates lots of ideas at a rapid pace
- First ideas typically aren't the best -- this forces you to think beyond those
- 1 minute per sketch isn't enough time to get bogged down in detail or perfection

How to do it:
1. Each person has a piece of paper folded into 8 sections
2. Set the timer for 8 minutes
3. Each person sketches one idea in each section
4. When the timer goes off, put pens down

After Crazy 8s:
- Ideas are reviewed to decide which to explore/develop further
- Shortlisted ideas can be progressed (more detail, scenarios, storyboards, future journey maps)
- Or do another round of Crazy 8s using top ideas as a starting point

### Evaluating early design ideas

Key questions:
- How do we know if our designs are useful?
- How do we choose between alternative ideas?
- How do we know if our designs have good usability?
- How do we know if they provide a positive user experience?
- How do we know if we need to make changes and what changes?
- How can we iterate in our design work?

Evaluation sits at every stage of the UCD process: evaluate existing systems (user research), evaluate ideas or early design prototypes (conceptual design), evaluate design prototypes (detailed design).

#### Purposes of evaluation
- Get early feedback on design concepts
- Decide between design options
- Check that users can use the product and whether they like it
- Identify and fix usability problems *before* production or release
- Identify and fix usability problems in existing products
- Assess competitor systems
- Focus on real problems -- not our intuition about what is good or bad

#### What can we evaluate?
- Sketches
- Paper prototypes
- Clickable digital prototypes
- Physical prototypes
- Wireframes
- Existing systems
- Competitor systems

#### How can we evaluate?
- Usability testing
- Expert evaluations (e.g. Heuristic Evaluation)
- Interviews
- Usability/UX questionnaires
- Design reviews and design critique

### Evaluating ideas (from Crazy 8s)

How do you decide which ideas to progress?
1. Each person presents their top 2 or 3 ideas back to the group
2. Vote on the best, e.g. using 'dot voting' (sticky dots)
3. See which ideas are most popular
- Then do another round of Crazy 8s or progress the ideas into more detailed designs

### Product Triad: Desirable, Viable, Feasible

- **Desirable (user)** -- useful and usable; alignment with user needs and desires
- **Feasible (technology)** -- budget, timeline, technical constraints
- **Viable (business)** -- financially sustainable in the longer term

The sweet spot is at the intersection of all three.

### Concept testing

- Present an idea, prototype, or concept to users to gather feedback
- Test concepts with users to validate (or abandon) ideas
- Get feedback on perceived value, usability flaws
- Use approaches such as surveys, focus groups, interviews
- This is *not* about testing for usability issues

### Design critique

A collaborative approach to reviewing ideas and designs. Can be undertaken throughout the design process; valuable in early design.

How?
- Presenter shares a design
- Critics give constructive feedback
- Facilitator manages the session

### Contextualising concepts

Conceptual design as envisionment -- envisioning a world in which the new interactive system or experience exists. Concerned with the "big picture":
- Overall paradigm or platform (e.g. web, mobile, AR/VR, voice)
- Main concepts, objects, and actions within the system
- Key activities and workflows that will be supported
- The division of responsibility between user and system

Contextualising involves imagining use in context:
- How might people interact with the system in the real world?
- How does interaction unfold over time?

| Dimension | Question |
| :--- | :--- |
| **User** | Who is involved / using the system? |
| **Context** | Where and when does the interaction happen? |
| **Activity** | What are they trying to achieve? |
| **Interaction / Journey** | How does the interaction unfold over time? |

Techniques for contextualising: scenarios, storyboards, future user journeys.

### Scenarios

A scenario is an *"informal narrative description"* of a user's task -- one typical story (John M. Carroll, 2000).

> "Scenarios are stories. They consist of a setting, or situation state, one or more actors with personal motivations, knowledge, and capabilities, and various tools and objects that the actors encounter and manipulate. The scenario describes a sequence of actions and events that lead to an outcome. These actions and events are related in a usage context that includes the goals, plans, and reactions of the people taking part in the episode." -- Rosson & Carroll (2012)

Key characteristics:
- Captures a task in user's language
- An informal, personalised, context-rich account of users, events, artefacts and environments
- Typically focuses on one persona
- Either current situations or (more likely) envisioned future situations
- Summarises user journeys; can have a collection of scenarios that encapsulate entire interaction with system
- Contains important concepts and actions

Scenarios should:
- Situate the use of the system
- Capture the user needs and goals
- Not dwell on unnecessary details of how the system will work (though some representation of technology is often necessary)
- Focus on how the system supports users in context

### Storyboards

An alternative representation of a user journey, but in visual form. Usually focuses on the user experience, not details of the design.

A storyboard contains:
- A sequence of panels or cells, each representing a single step in the user's journey
- Captions to explain the content of each panel
- Speech / thought bubbles to show users' thoughts / motivations
- A clear 'beginning' and 'end'

Artistic quality is not important!

Storyboards in conceptual design:
- Similar to scenarios but in visual form -- very compact way of showing interaction in context
- Highly creative; good for communicating key aspects of system
- Very similar to storyboarding in film
- A series of panels showing the major interaction moments with the system
- Show important concepts, actions, and context of use
- Use to expose proposed features of the system, showing their use in context

Developing a storyboard:
1. Start with a particular persona who has a particular goal (gives overall purpose; different personas will behave differently)
2. Work out the scenario or journey -- decide on a starting point and ending point, work out key steps to illustrate between them, decide on the point of view for each step/frame
3. Create a panel with a caption and dialogue / thought bubbles for each step
4. Try a storyboard for one persona, then see if the story varies for others
5. Explore different journeys and different digital touchpoints

### Future user journey maps

Similar to the current ('as-is') user journey maps from Session 4, BUT instead of describing the *current* experience discovered through research, they describe a *possible future experience enabled by a new design*.

Future journey maps:
- Show how an experience could unfold with the new system
- Explore stages of interaction over time
- Help designers think about how a design changes the overall experience

| Current journey map | Future journey map |
| :--- | :--- |
| Describes the existing experience | Describes a possible future experience |
| Based on user research | Based on design ideas / concepts |
| Identifies problems and opportunities | Explores how the design could improve the experience |

You can use a current journey map as a starting point / inspiration for a future journey map -- to explore how things might change (i.e. improve) as a result of the new design / system.

### Mental models

Mental models are representations of the external world that people construct in their minds to help them understand how things work and what to do in current and future situations.

> "People create mental models of themselves, others, the environment and the things with which they interact. These are conceptual models, formed through experience, training and instruction. These models serve as guides to help achieve our goals and in understanding the world." -- Don Norman (2013), *The Design of Everyday Things*

Mental models and interaction design:
- Users need to understand how to interact with complex (and not so complex) digital technologies
- *"Most people have incomplete, unstable, easily confusable and superstitious mental models of systems and devices"* (Norman, 1983)
- Interaction problems arise when a person's mental model differs from the reality in ways that impact their interaction with a system
- Users may have a mental model of the task they want to do (particularly if they have done it before with a different technology)
- But other users may have different mental models!
- Part of the challenge of designing good user interfaces is to design for lots of users with different mental models

#### Norman's three models
- **Designer's model** -- the designer's internal conception of the product
- **System model/image** -- what has been created that can be perceived (product + documentation)
- **User's model** -- the user's conception (mental model) of the product, formed from interacting with the system image
